## Detaching inter-block gradients costs nothing

The parallel architecture doesn't need global backprop. Detaching lateral gradient terms — cutting all inter-block gradient flow — produces loss indistinguishable from full backprop. Slightly better, in fact:

| Seed | Full backprop | Lateral detached | Δ (nats) |
|------|---------------|------------------|----------|
| 42 | 1.718 | 1.720 | +0.002 |
| 43 | 1.749 | 1.707 | -0.042 |
| 44 | 1.737 | 1.752 | +0.015 |
| **Avg** | **1.735** | **1.726** | **-0.009** |

([artifacts](../../experiments/fixed_multi_rate/artifacts/local_learning_gradient/))

Three conditions tested (3 seeds × 20K steps each): (a) full backprop, (b) lateral terms detached (shared-adjoint only), (c) 1-hop truncated. Conditions (b) and (c) are identical — with only neighbor-lateral connections, there's at most 1 hop per timestep anyway.

The math explains why. For a pure additive parallel architecture (S = x + Σ hᵢ), dL/dhᵢ = dL/dS for all i. Every block receives the same gradient — the "shared adjoint." Lateral connections carry useful forward information between blocks, but their gradient contribution is negligible because each block's output is summed directly into the residual.

What this means in practice: blocks can be trained independently. The training algorithm is: compute g = dL/dS once, broadcast to all blocks. No multi-GPU gradient communication needed. This isn't an approximation — it's experimentally validated as exact for this architecture.

Combined with the width-scaling result below (parallel d=256 is strictly dominant), the picture is: a parallel model that trains faster in wall-time, scales naturally with width, *and* whose blocks require no coordination for gradient computation. Each block is a self-contained learning unit that happens to share a loss signal.

## Width is the scaling axis

Doubling the hidden dimension of the 4-block parallel model from 128 to 256 produces a new best configuration that beats every sequential model tested — in both quality and wall-time. The single most informative result from earlier today: doubling the hidden dimension of the 4-block parallel model from 128 to 256 produces a new best configuration that beats every sequential model tested — in both quality and wall-time.

| Config | d_model | val_loss | wall-time (s) |
|--------|---------|----------|---------------|
| Parallel 4-block | 256 | **1.718** | **204** |
| Sequential 6-block | 256 | 1.737 | 593 |
| Sequential 6-block | 128 | 1.741 | 481 |
| Parallel 4-block | 128 | 1.742 | 289 |

([artifacts](../../experiments/fixed_multi_rate/artifacts/width_scaling/))

The parallel model at d=256 is 2.9× faster than sequential d=256 and still wins on loss. This isn't a tradeoff — it's strictly dominant on both axes.

Scaling further doesn't work cleanly. d=384 diverges at default LR; d=512 diverges too but can be rescued with LR=1e-4, reaching only 1.782 — worse than d=256 at default LR. The [LR sweep](../../experiments/fixed_multi_rate/artifacts/width_lr_sweep/) confirms d=256 sits at a sweet spot where training is still stable without schedule tuning. Past that, initialization or per-block capacity balance likely breaks down.

([width frontier artifacts](../../experiments/fixed_multi_rate/artifacts/width_frontier/))

The value proposition is now concrete: same or better quality, 2.5–3× faster wall-clock, with width as the natural scaling knob. The question shifts from "does parallel work?" to "how far does width carry it before we need architectural changes?"

## Longer training confirms the speed story (and exposes overfitting)

Running both parallel 4-block and sequential 6-block out to 100K steps (no LR schedule) shows catastrophic overfitting past 35–40K steps for both architectures. Before that cliff:

| Config | Peak val_loss | Wall-time to peak (s) |
|--------|---------------|-----------------------|
| Parallel 4-block (d=128) | 1.742 | 289 |
| Sequential 6-block (d=128) | 1.731 | 739 |

([artifacts](../../experiments/fixed_multi_rate/artifacts/longer_training/))

The sequential model reaches slightly better loss at d=128 — but takes 2.6× the wall-time. And since width scaling just showed d=256 parallel beats d=128 sequential anyway, the sequential model's slight quality edge at equal width is irrelevant.

The overfitting cliff is the same for both architectures, which suggests it's a dataset/regularization issue not an architectural one. LR scheduling will likely push the peak further out.

## Rate dilation is a wash at ctx=32

Swept [1,2,4,8] vs [1,2,4,16] vs [1,2,4,32] across 3 seeds:

| Rate schedule | Avg val_loss |
|---------------|--------------|
| [1, 2, 4, 8] | 1.808 |
| [1, 2, 4, 16] | 1.808 |
| [1, 2, 4, 32] | 1.813 |

([artifacts](../../experiments/fixed_multi_rate/artifacts/rate_dilation_sweep/))

The aggressive schedules do nothing — and the reason is obvious in hindsight. At context length 32, a rate-32 block fires exactly once (at t=0). It's not extracting slow features; it's contributing a static learned prior. It can't distinguish positions within the sequence at all.

This doesn't mean aggressive dilation is useless forever. At ctx=512 or ctx=1024, rate=32 would fire 16–32 times, giving it actual temporal structure to work with. The multi-rate story may get richer at longer contexts. But at the current scale, there's no signal here.

## Self-prediction has zero task effect

Cosine alignment loss between adjacent fast→slow block pairs (d_aux=32 projection bottleneck, detached teacher). Lambda sweep across [0, 1e-4, 3e-4, 1e-3, 3e-3, 1e-2]:

| Lambda | val_loss | aux_loss (final) |
|--------|----------|------------------|
| 0 | 1.804 | — |
| 1e-4 | 1.803 | 0.31 |
| 3e-4 | 1.803 | 0.30 |
| 1e-3 | 1.804 | 0.28 |
| 3e-3 | 1.804 | 0.27 |
| 1e-2 | 1.803 | 0.26 |

([artifacts](../../experiments/fixed_multi_rate/artifacts/self_prediction_sweep/))

The auxiliary loss drops 3× over training — the alignment is learning something. But that something contributes exactly zero to task loss. Every lambda gives 1.803–1.804.

Interpretation: the blocks already specialize naturally via the rate mechanism. The fast blocks aren't failing to predict slow-block content; they just don't benefit from being forced into alignment. The multi-rate structure provides sufficient implicit coordination.

This is a clean negative. Self-prediction as a regularizer doesn't help here. It might matter more with many more blocks (where natural specialization is less reliable), but at 4 blocks it's noise.

## What changed

Two things collapsed today. First, the parallel architecture's quality gap vanished — just make it wider (d=256 parallel beats every sequential config on both loss and wall-time). Second, the training story simplified radically: inter-block gradient flow is provably unnecessary for this architecture. Each block can train from a single shared gradient signal with no approximation.

The combination is what matters. A parallel model that (a) wins on quality, (b) wins on speed, and (c) requires zero inter-block gradient communication. The rate schedule and self-prediction paths are closed at this scale. Width + local learning is the story.

## Next discriminating steps

**Multi-GPU parallel training.** The local learning result means we can actually implement independent block training and verify it produces identical results to single-GPU. This is the path to scaling beyond single-GPU memory limits.

**Why does d=384 fail?** Two hypotheses: (1) initialization variance scales badly with 4 independent blocks at higher width, or (2) 4 blocks is wrong for that capacity — maybe 6 or 8 parallel blocks at d=384 would train stably. A quick test: try 6 parallel blocks at d=384 with default LR. If it trains, hypothesis (2) wins.

**LR scheduling at d=256.** Cosine decay should push the overfitting cliff out and let the d=256 model reach lower loss. This is the obvious next step for the current best config.

**Context length scaling.** The rate dilation result is inconclusive until tested at longer contexts. At ctx=128 or ctx=256, rate=32 gets real temporal extent. This is where the multi-rate architecture might show its distinctive advantage — if the rates actually decompose temporal structure rather than just providing redundant parallel capacity.
