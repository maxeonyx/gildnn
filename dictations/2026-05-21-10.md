# Dictation 2026-05-21-10

I've just remembered a really clever and clean thing that I did in my master's work. Instead of at the end of a transformer — you have a sequence of tokens, text tokens — by default a GPT-style model would implicitly predict the next token in the sequence. So the input would be token t and the output is implicitly t plus one.

Something I had done in my master's work is arbitrary order sampling. Training on a dataset where instead of always predicting the next token, you get given the query for the next prediction — i.e. the positional encoding — and then get asked to produce not necessarily the next token, but a token sometime in the future in the sequence. There were various orders I used and randomization. The key part being at the very end of the transformer network, instead of doing a self-attention, doing a cross-attention from the sequence to the target position.

The cool thing is that this can also be used for special tokens or special outputs with no extra work. Instead of having the network predict a loss or a stop yes/no token at the next step, you can give it a question — "stop now, yes or no?" — and it can choose a token yes or no, as long as you've trained that in. But most importantly, you can give it "what is the next thing in the sequence?" or "what is three tokens ahead in the sequence?" or "what should I add back in the sequence?" — and you don't have to redo the KV cache or anything like that.

I find it quite elegant. It's a property of the dataset — you need to train it in, otherwise it can only predict the next token anyway — but it's unlocked by attention. I did this on MNIST pixels and found the results quite interesting. I trained it to predict in raster order and random orders, and at inference time you could then choose an order. You could plan in an optimal order, potentially — although I only did some heuristic orders.

I think it's quite interesting: if you can choose the order that you sample tokens, can you do better sampling than just going forward in time? I think you can. I definitely think you can.

The dataset for text is kind of tricky to imagine, but the dataset for other things makes sense. Like 3D video patches — if you've got a ball bouncing through a scene, you can predict that quite easily, but if you've got trees wobbling around in the background, well maybe you don't predict those until the very end. You can choose to predict in an order that makes sense given what you already know.

On MNIST, whatever orders I trained on worked well, and training on a single order worked better than training on many orders — specialization beats generalization, at least at that scale.

I tried sampling by high entropy and by low entropy and got distributions skewed towards black pixels or towards white pixels respectively.

One nice demo: you could plug in one pixel and get the conditional marginal distributions of all the other pixels based on that one pixel — in a single forward pass, not one forward pass per pixel. That's because it's cross-attention to the query position, so all marginals come out simultaneously. That gives you something like a Gaussian process — a very powerful object — which is quite hard to get out of a standard autoregressive model.

I feel like the modality of text does a disservice to the flexibility of transformers. Text is sequential by nature so it pushes you toward causal left-to-right modeling, and those constraints get baked into the architecture assumptions — causal masking, next-token prediction, KV cache designed for sequential generation. Then people treat those as fundamental properties of transformers rather than choices made for one modality. But the attention mechanism itself has no preference for sequence order — it's just learned similarity between query and key vectors, completely order-agnostic until you impose positional structure. The arbitrary order sampling idea makes that explicit.

Some cool things you can do with arbitrary order sampling: you can use typed query tokens and put in a dataset from a relational database and sample records in an order-independent fashion — autoregressively though. You learn based on a set of records, randomize the order that you get the network to predict in training, and then at inference time you can choose any next part to sample from and get the correct distribution in theory over that next thing.

I believe I saw some paper from Google doing this on hyperparameters for some kind of optimization in the cloud — they essentially did a massive ablation, plugged the parameters into this kind of Gaussian process training, and got out a neural representation that could be used to plan. Super interesting stuff.
