# Dictation 2026-05-21-6

**Max:** It seems to me like neural networks should be complex-valued. That's kind of what I think.

**Assistant:** What's your intuition for why — is it primarily the stability argument, norm preservation, or something else?

**Max:** Yeah, a stability argument, norm preservation, basically. If we have a complex-valued neural network, we can apply transformations to it more cleanly.

**Assistant:** The strong version of the argument: if all weight matrices are unitary, every layer is an isometry — activations neither grow nor shrink. You get stability essentially for free, without needing the optimizer or norms to compensate. The tension is that nonlinearities break unitarity between layers, so the stability guarantee is partial.

**Max:** I don't care about the network being linear. I don't care about each individual activation keeping its norm, but I care about the distribution. Let's assume that the data distribution is approximately normally distributed — and in fact for embeddings we basically have that. Then if that is the case, we want transformations that keep the distribution relatively normally distributed. The norm of the distribution is more important. I don't know if that's a thing that makes sense.

**Assistant:** It does make sense. If your inputs are isotropic Gaussian, a unitary/orthogonal transformation maps them to another isotropic Gaussian with the same variance — the distribution shape is exactly preserved. Non-orthogonal transformations distort the covariance: some directions get amplified, others compressed. That's where you get the pathological distributions that norms and optimizers are trying to correct after the fact. Your intuition is: parameterize so the distortion never happens in the first place, rather than correcting it downstream.

**Max:** Ideally we wouldn't be doing element-wise nonlinearities in my opinion. That has a privileged basis.

**Assistant:** Sharp observation. Element-wise nonlinearities pick out the standard basis as special — which is arbitrary and breaks the rotational symmetry that unitary transformations would otherwise preserve. If you rotate your representation and then apply element-wise ReLU, you get something different than if you applied ReLU first. The nonlinearity is basis-dependent. If you want the full picture to be basis-independent, you'd want a nonlinearity that operates on the geometry of the representation, not on individual coordinates.

**Max:** Attention's not really a nonlinearity. Well, is it? Let's say we rotate a distribution and add it back — rotate it and then add it, a multi-dimensional rotation. Then what we get out is a nonlinearity? Essentially a non-linear combination. I don't think it really is. No, I'm more thinking of like some kind of a flow. Like rotating the top of a sphere one way and the bottom of a sphere another way.

**Assistant:** Attention is technically nonlinear because of the softmax, but it's not really serving the role of a nonlinearity in the way activations do. Your sphere idea is describing something like a vector field on a manifold, where different parts of the space get rotated differently depending on where they are — essentially a flow. A smooth, invertible, data-dependent transformation. The transformation is nonlinear in the sense that it's data-dependent, but it's locally a rotation/isometry, so it preserves the local geometry. That would be basis-independent by construction.

**Max:** I don't know what the high-dimensional version of that is. A vector field, yeah. Basically moving around the data points within the sphere without moving around the distribution.

**Assistant:** Right — so the constraint is that the transformation preserves the distribution, not just individual norms. A volume-preserving diffeomorphism. In high dimensions that's a vector field with zero divergence — divergence-free, so no net expansion or contraction anywhere.

The relevant math:

- **Volume-preserving maps**: a smooth map f: R^n → R^n preserves volume iff det(Jf) = 1 everywhere, where Jf is the Jacobian. Orthogonal/unitary linear maps are a special case (det = ±1).
- **Divergence-free vector fields**: if you think of the transformation as a flow along a vector field v(x), volume is preserved iff div(v) = 0. This is the incompressible fluid condition.
- **Hamiltonian flows**: in physics, Hamiltonian dynamics is volume-preserving by Liouville's theorem. The vector field is determined by a scalar "energy" function H(x): v = J∇H where J is the symplectic matrix. This gives a principled way to parameterize volume-preserving flows via a learned H.
- **Coupling layers / normalizing flows**: practical ML approximations. NICE, RealNVP etc. build volume-preserving maps out of simple pieces, but tend to be basis-dependent in construction.

**Max:** Somehow I don't understand why it's so hard to define a divergence-free vector field in a parameterized way. Like okay, let's say I have a neural network that is trained to produce a transformation that is a divergence-free vector field, or whatever, a volume-preserving map, a Hamiltonian flow. Can we train a neural network to implement such a transformation? Then if we can do that, can we use that as the nonlinearity? And then that neural network itself can have privileged bases.

**Assistant:** Yes, you can. Parameterize a scalar function H(x) with a neural network — which can have whatever internal structure it likes, privileged bases and all — then derive the vector field as v = J∇H. That's automatically divergence-free by construction. You apply that flow as your "nonlinearity." The outer network is basis-independent in the sense that matters — its effect on representations is defined geometrically. The inner network that parameterizes H is just a function approximator; its internal structure doesn't matter. The practical issue is that actually running the flow requires integrating the ODE dx/dt = v(x), which is expensive — you need something like a neural ODE solver. But it's not fundamentally impossible. So the picture would be: unitary linear layers + Hamiltonian-flow nonlinearities. Both preserve the distribution. Neither privileges a basis in the representation space. The inner network computing H is just a tool, not part of the representation.
